# Perspective writing: building an insight that changes how the reader thinks

Version 1.2.1; updated 2026-11-23. Read when writing or rewriting a Nature/Science/Cell-style Perspective, a conceptual review, or a theory/opinion piece. Pair with editorial-and-submission.md, innovation-and-broad-interest.md and manuscript-production.md.

## 1. The test of a Perspective

After reading, a scientifically informed reader should understand something differently: a relationship, a mechanism, a limitation of current framing, or a question that is now visible. A topic, a list of open problems, or a summary of a field is not yet a Perspective.

Write one private editorial sentence:

> After this article, a reader will understand that [relationship/mechanism/framing] is [different] because [the conceptual move], with consequences for [research/measurement/design].

If that sentence is vague, the piece has no intellectual centre yet.

## 2. Find the intellectual centre

- Start from a concrete biological tension, anomaly, contradiction or failure of current framing; not from an abstract noun ("geometry", "representation", "landscape") or a technology list.
- Name the conceptual move: a new distinction, a causal relation, a measurement principle, a design rule or a reframing that resolves the tension.
- State what the move changes: which question becomes answerable, which interpretation becomes untenable, which experiment becomes worth doing.
- Attribute inherited concepts and prior engineering successes fairly; novelty is the relation or consequence, not adjectives.
- Choose one or two recurring examples that accumulate insight. Additional examples must do distinct argumentative work.

## 3. Design the argument as a sequence of discoveries

A useful progression (adapt to the subject):

1. An unexplained observation or a tension in current understanding.
2. Why existing framings cannot resolve it.
3. The proposed conceptual move, stated plainly.
4. A concrete system/example that exposes the move.
5. What follows for measurement, experiment or design.
6. Boundaries, counterpositions and what would change the author's mind.
7. Consequences for the field and a small set of testable next steps.

Each section should deliver a distinct intellectual result. At its end, the reader should have a reason to ask the next question. Do not repeat the central idea unless the new context adds an implication.

## 4. Make paragraphs explain

- Open with an intelligible claim or observation; develop the causal/conceptual reasoning; place a concrete example or evidence where it helps; state the consequence.
- Prefer actor–action–object: "mutate the sequence, recompute its representation and ask which measured response changes" rather than "operation-conditioned representation fidelity assessment".
- Introduce a term after the reader has met the problem it names. Retain names that do repeated work; let other ideas use ordinary language.
- Use analogies for an exact explanatory purpose; state the correspondence through the scientific example. Do not infer physical identity, energy or causality from an analogy.
- Avoid stock transitions, hype, invented compounds, noun strings and conspicuous "not X but Y" formulas. State the claim affirmatively.

## 5. Accountability and speculation

- Mark status locally: established fact (declarative, with evidence), interpretation ("we propose", "this suggests"), hypothesis ("we hypothesise"), future possibility ("could", "would").
- Make an ambitious possibility specific: what relationship might be learned, which observation could reveal it, how it would change an experiment.
- Place qualifications at the inference they constrain, then show the question or measurement that follows. Do not let successive warnings become the article's structure.
- Separate model output, existing experimental result and proposed experiment. A conceptual diagram must not resemble measured success.
- A published hypothesis is evidence that its authors proposed it, not that it is settled. A cross-species analogy is not a measured scaling law.

## 6. Mathematics and formal content

- Choose formulas for the conceptual work they do; define variables and units before or immediately after each equation.
- State the assumptions needed for a displayed implication; keep established foundations attributed.
- Do not inflate an elementary bound or a restatement of sufficiency into the article's main discovery.
- For learned biological representations, avoid unsupported identities: embedding geometry with a unique physical manifold; sequence-prefix order with folding time; ensemble samples with trajectories; equilibrium occupancy with rates; inverse covariance with curvature; model change with coordinate transformation; a metric with a beneficial intervention.
- Explain the constructive alternative in the biological paragraph, not in a technical aside.

## 7. Evidence and citation discipline

- Build a claim ledger for the central steps: claim → evidence → source → status → boundary.
- Prefer primary research for factual results; use Perspectives for attributed definitions, syntheses or agendas.
- Cite close prior work even when it limits apparent novelty; verify bibliographic identity and preprint status.
- Keep a result within the conditions tested; do not let an impressive example validate a platform.
- Use references where the reasoning occurs, not as a catalogue at the start.

## 8. Figures and boxes

- Figures should make an operation, relationship or causal structure visible; a conceptual figure is not a data figure and must be labelled as conceptual.
- Use boxes for compact formalisation, definitions or necessary technical background that would interrupt the argument.
- Keep the visual vocabulary consistent with the paper's terms and colour roles; no decorative schematics.

## 9. Bilingual Perspective

Complete and verify the English argument first, then produce the aligned Chinese version unless the user requests another order. Preserve claims, evidence status, citations, mathematical meaning, emphasis and section/figure correspondence. Write natural Chinese paragraphs; do not import English noun chains. Return each translated paragraph to its counterpart to check omissions and changes in certainty.

## 10. Perspective audit

- Can the central insight be stated in one sentence, and does the article deliver it?
- Does each section produce a new intellectual result rather than repeat the thesis?
- Is the concrete example doing explanatory work, not decoration?
- Are established facts, interpretations, hypotheses and proposed experiments marked correctly?
- Are counterpositions and boundary conditions taken seriously?
- Are equations and analogies used only where they clarify, with assumptions stated?
- Does the abstract work as a miniature argument, not a topic summary?
- Are citations verified and placed where the reasoning needs them?
- Could a biologist outside the subfield follow the argument without a new glossary?
- Does the ending state consequences and testable next steps rather than praise?
