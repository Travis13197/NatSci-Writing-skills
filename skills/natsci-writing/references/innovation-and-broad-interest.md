# Innovation, contribution and broad interest

Version 1.1.0; updated 2026-11-23. Read when framing a study, testing whether it is more than incremental, writing a significance statement or cover letter, or preparing for editorial triage. This file turns "novelty" into auditable scientific claims.

## 1. What counts as an advance

Classify the contribution before writing the claim:

| Contribution type | What must be new | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|
| Phenomenon | A previously unobserved or unmeasured phenomenon | Direct observation, controls, replication |
| Mechanism | A causal explanation that survives competing explanations | Perturbation, rescue, epistasis, orthogonal measurement |
| Measurement / capability | A method that enables a previously inaccessible measurement | Validation against truth, baselines, failure modes |
| Resource | A dataset, atlas, benchmark or material of broad reuse value | Coverage, quality control, baselines, access |
| Theory / principle | A rule that predicts or unifies observations | Derivation, prediction, out-of-sample test |
| Clinical / translational | A change in diagnosis, treatment or decision-making | Effect size, safety, prospective or independent validation |

A paper can combine types, but the abstract and cover letter must name the primary one. "We built a framework" is not a contribution type until the capability and evidence are named.

## 2. The contribution ladder

Place the work on the ladder and state honestly which rung it reaches:

1. **Incremental** — a known effect in a new dataset, model or setting; useful, but not an editorial advance.
2. **Discriminating** — a result that separates competing explanations or defines a boundary.
3. **Explanatory** — a mechanism or principle that accounts for several observations.
4. **Enabling** — a measurement or capability that makes new questions answerable.
5. **Field-changing** — redraws what the field takes as established or makes a class of problems tractable.

Most Nature/Science papers need rung 2–4 in a broad frame; rung 5 is rare and must not be claimed without evidence. The ladder is a diagnostic, not a label to print.

## 3. Gap engineering

A publishable gap is a scientific unknown, not the absence of your method.

Weak gap: "No one has applied our model to this task."
Stronger gap: "Performance improves in some settings but degrades in others; which condition determines whether the proposed mechanism helps?"

Build the gap from:
- an anomaly that existing explanations cannot absorb;
- a contradiction between two credible bodies of evidence;
- a boundary where a claimed effect reverses or disappears;
- an inaccessible measurement that blocks a class of questions;
- a scale or condition that current designs cannot test;
- a cost, failure or fairness constraint that changes practice.

Test the gap:
- Can a sceptical reader state the unknown in one sentence?
- Is it genuinely unknown, disputed or untested, rather than merely unaddressed by you?
- Would resolving it change an explanation, a measurement or a decision?
- Does the study design actually discriminate the proposed answer from alternatives?

## 4. Discriminating predictions

For each central claim, write the competing explanations and the observation that separates them:

| Explanation | Prediction if true | Prediction if false | Test in this study |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | | |

If two explanations predict the same observation, the study cannot distinguish them. Add a perturbation, a boundary condition or an orthogonal measurement. A result that is consistent with the hypothesis but also with every alternative is not evidence for the hypothesis.

## 5. Broad-interest test

Nature/Science editors ask who outside the specialty will care and why. Answer concretely:

- Which field, technology, policy or clinical decision does the result change?
- What can researchers now do, predict or measure that they could not before?
- Does the result connect two areas, resolve a long-standing disagreement, or set a boundary that practitioners need?
- Would a non-specialist understand the consequence from the abstract alone?

A broad-interest claim must not be a generic promise ("advances the field", "opens new avenues"). Name the audience and the change.

## 6. Why this, why now, why broad

Write three sentences and test them:
1. **Why this** — the exact unknown and why it matters.
2. **Why now** — the new data, method, instrument or convergence that makes the question answerable now.
3. **Why broad** — who beyond the specialty gains what.

If any sentence requires adjectives rather than nouns and evidence, the framing is not ready.

## 7. Incremental work, honestly framed

Incremental results can still be publishable when they:
- establish a boundary that changes interpretation;
- provide the first adequate measurement of an important quantity;
- replicate or refute a widely relied-upon claim with stronger design;
- enable a capability that others can reuse;
- connect a mechanism to a real-world decision.

Do not inflate rung 1 into rung 4 with vocabulary. State the bounded advance, then let the evidence carry it.

## 8. Positioning against prior work

Build a closest-prior-work table:

| Study | Question | Design | Main claim | What it could not establish | Difference in this work |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Use it to write one paragraph that positions the work fairly: what prior work established, what remained unresolved, and what this study adds. Do not manufacture novelty by flattening prior work into a weak baseline.

## 9. Idea generation that produces testable advances

- **Tension mapping**: list pairs of credible findings that cannot both be fully true; design a test that separates them.
- **Anomaly hunting**: identify results that are reported as noise or exceptions; ask whether they share a condition.
- **Method-as-question**: for every new capability, ask which old question it can now answer decisively.
- **Scale and condition sweep**: test whether a claim holds across size, dose, time, domain or data regime; boundaries are contributions.
- **Cross-domain transfer**: import a mechanism or measurement from another field and test whether it predicts here.
- **Reverse engineering**: start from a desired decision or capability and identify the measurement that would justify it.
- **Red team**: write the strongest reviewer objection before drafting, then design the experiment or analysis that answers it.

## 10. Innovation audit

- Is the primary contribution type named?
- Is the gap a scientific unknown rather than a missing method?
- Are competing explanations stated with discriminating predictions?
- Does each central claim have evidence that would change if the claim were false?
- Is the broad-interest audience named with a concrete change?
- Are why-this, why-now and why-broad answerable without adjectives?
- Is prior work fairly represented, with a clear unresolved question?
- Does the title/abstract claim match the strongest evidence and its boundary?
